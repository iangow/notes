How common are direct-method cash flow statements?

A tutorial on working with XBRL data compiled using dera.pq

Author

Ian D. Gow

Published

11 June 2026

Modified

23 October 2026

Abstract

This note illustrates how data compiled using my dera.pq R package can be used to analyse XBRL data from SEC filings. Using these data, I show that direct-method cash flow statements have become even rarer for US firms than they were in 2011, when the last Accounting Trends and Techniques survey was published.

PDF version (11 June 2026)

1 Introduction

Many filings with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) are tagged using XBRL (eXtensible Business Reporting Language), a global framework for encoding and sharing business information. The SEC’s Division of Economic and Risk Analysis (DERA) provides bulk files compiled from these filings and my recently published R package, dera.pq, is designed to facilitate fast analysis of these data sets. This note provides a case study of how data compiled with the dera.pq can be used and provides a kind of tutorial on parts of the XBRL data set provided by DERA.

The specific question I explore here is: How many US firms provide direct-method cash flow statements? Accounting Trends and Techniques, an AICPA publication, was long the source for an answer to this question and it typically reported that about 1% of firms used the direct method. However, the effective demise of Accounting Trends and Techniques in 2011 has deprived us of an easy answer to this question.

Can we get an answer from XBRL filings? Well, yes. While it is surprisingly difficult to corral the messy, error-ridden XBRL data provided by individual filers into a clean answer, it seems that the headline 1% number is out of date. During the period I study, the popularity(?) of direct-method cash flow statements has been in steady decline and today perhaps 0.2% of firms use direct-method cash flow statements.

Despite their rarity, the belief that direct-method cash flow statements would be useful to investors seems to persist. As recently as December 2023, the Chief Accountant of the SEC issued a call for more firms to use the direct method. Interestingly, even the SEC Chief Accountant appears to rely on statistics from no later than 2002 to describe the state of play regarding direct-method reporting by firms. So perhaps more recent data would be helpful.

1.1 Some observations on the SEC’s XBRL data

While my initial goal with this note was the illustration of how data collated with my dera.pq package can be used, a side effect of of preparing this note is that I am able to make some observations on the utility of the XBRL data based on my experience. While these observations are based on my limited use of the data in preparing this note, I conjecture that they may hold up in a more comprehensive analysis.

First, I view the XBRL data as having greatest utility when analysing longer time periods or comparing larger samples of firms. In April 2025, the SEC published some Python code illustrating the use of DERA data. However, these examples typically quickly move to looking at particular fields for a particular firm for a small number of periods (say, two quarters). If that were the goal, there is arguably little value in using XBRL at all; one can quickly go to the relevant filing and view the few required numbers in context and quickly type them into Excel.1 But the approach suggested in the SEC code would quickly become unworkable for the kinds of analyses for which having XBRL would be more useful.2 In this regard, the dera.pq package provides a better approach.

Second, the minimum quality of the XBRL data is disappointingly low. For bulk analyses, it is probably the lower bound of data quality that is most relevant. Having 90% of the data above a certain quality does not help with comprehensive analyses if the other 10% cannot be ignored. If the bad 10% needs to be included, then a lot of work is needed to patch it up, likely much more than dealing with the better 90%. Even then, one’s confidence in the resulting data will be lower than if greater assurance had been built into the process of producing it.

While I use the data in a very limited way in this note, data issues plague my analysis.3 Many firms do not provide the data explaining how various data points are aggregated and organized in a way that can be understood by a computer. On top of this, there are too many tags provided by the US GAAP taxonomy and many firms use custom tags, seemingly without good reason. I have seen many incorrectly tagged items in my limited use.

Finally, while one might not choose the general approach of tagging used in XBRL if starting with a clean sheet of paper, I think that the fundamental technology is perfectly workable. Harris and Morsfield (2012) suggest that XBRL was out of date even in 2012. However, I have found working with the XBRL data in the form created using the dera.pq package based on the files supplied by DERA to be fast and facile. The much bigger problem is that the quality of the data is too low.

Given how long the XBRL project has been around and how poor the data quality appears to be, I must say I would assess a low likelihood that the XBRL data in SEC filings will be improved any time soon, if ever. The solution seems straightforward in principle: there should be some assurance around the XBRL data in terms of how the items are tagged, how they relate to each other, and how taxonomies are used. This is a solution that would have been viable fifteen years ago and clearly the decision was made not to take XBRL data seriously. As such, it’s unclear that anything fundamental has changed to make it more attractive to invest time, money, and political capital in improving the quality of data that surely few really use.

Even in our current AI age, this seems to be a shame, because I conjecture that what AI can do with high-quality curated data sets is quite different from what it can do if dealing with messy, dirty data of the kind currently seen in the XBRL data set provided by the SEC.4

2 Background

2.1 Cash flow reporting under US GAAP

US GAAP has required most public firms to provide statements of cash flows since FAS 95, which was issued by the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) in 1987. When the FASB created the Accounting Standards Codification (ASC) in 2009, the substance of FAS 95 became ASC Topic 230, Statement of Cash Flows.

ASC 230-10-45-24 requires a statement of cash flows that reports net cash provided or used by operating, investing, and financing activities. In effect, the statement of cash flows reconciles beginning and ending balances of cash and cash equivalents.5

ASC 230-10-45-25 says (emphasis added) “entities are encouraged to report … net cash flow from operating activities [using] the direct method.” 230-10-45-30 requires that a firm that “uses the direct method” provide a “reconciliation of net income to net cash flow from operating activities shall be provided in a separate schedule.”

2.2 Accounting Trends and Techniques

Despite being “encouraged” to report cash flow from operating activities using the direct method, Mulford and Comiskey (2005, p. 52) report that “only about 1 percent of firms present the statement of cash flows in the direct format.” The statistic reported by Mulford and Comiskey (2005) comes from the much-cited Accounting Trends and Techniques, which was an annual publication of the American Institute of Certified Public Accountants (AICPA), the principal professional association for certified public accountants (CPAs) in the United States.

Mulford and Comiskey (2005) cite the 2002 edition of Accounting Trends and Techniques, which surveyed 600 firms for its statistics. It appears that the 2008 edition of Accounting Trends and Techniques, was the last to cover 600 firms. From 2009 to 2011, the annual survey covered 500 firms. Starting in 2012, the Accounting Trends and Techniques title was changed, the annual survey dropped to 350 firms and statistical tables were omitted.6 In effect, the annual survey element of Accounting Trends and Techniques ceased in 2011.

2.3 XBRL data

With the demise of Accounting Trends and Techniques, it is not clear that anyone is compiling statistics on the prevalence of the indirect method for reporting cash flows from operating activities.7

However, such a survey may be unnecessary as, in January 2009, the SEC adopted rules requiring public companies to furnish financial statements in eXtensible Business Reporting Language (XBRL). XBRL was developed in the late 1990s under the auspices of what became XBRL International. The US effort was led by XBRL US, which worked with the Securities and Exchange Commission to develop GAAP taxonomies. While the SEC launched a voluntary XBRL filing program in 2005, only a small number of firms participated and data quality was often poor. By mid-2011, essentially all public companies were filing XBRL exhibits with their 10-Ks and 10-Qs. In 2018, the SEC adopted rules requiring Inline XBRL (iXBRL) in which human-readable filings and XBRL tags are combined and today essentially all SEC filers use Inline XBRL.

The SEC publishes two bulk XBRL data libraries that are useful for financial statement research: Financial Statement and Financial Statement and Notes. The latter SEC website explains that “the Financial Statement and Notes Data Sets … provide the text and detailed numeric information from all financial statements and their notes … in a flattened format to help users analyze and compare corporate disclosure information over time and across registrants.”

The Financial Statement and Notes data library comprises eight tables:

  • sub includes one record for each XBRL submission. It also includes fields pertinent to the submission and the filing entity.
  • tag contains all standard taxonomy tags (not just those appearing in submissions to date) and all custom taxonomy tags defined in the submissions. The standard tags are derived from taxonomies in the SEC’s standard taxonomies file as of the date of submission.
  • dim contains all of the combinations of XBRL axis and member used to tag any submission.
  • num contains numeric data, one row per data point in the financial statements.
  • txt contains non-numeric data, one row per data point in the financial statements.
  • ren summarizes “rendering” the data provided by filers about each presentation group as defined in EDGAR filer manual.
  • pre contains one row for each line of the financial statements tagged by the filer.
  • cal is the calculation data set and provides information about arithmetic relations between tagged amounts in a filing.

My dera.pq package downloads those zip files and writes their component tables as Parquet files in a local Parquet repository. More information about making a Parquet repository is found in Empirical Research in Accounting: Tools and Methods, which has both R and Python editions.

3 Analysis

3.1 Extracting cash flow data from XBRL data

In this version of the note, I use R to analyse the data. Specifically, I use the Tidyverse packages along with DuckDB. DuckDB allows me to spin up a fast and powerful database process using a single line of code. I access the DERA Notes data using the load_parquet() function from my farr package, which understands the structure of my Parquet data repository.

In my analysis for this note, I primarily use three tables from those described above, namely, sub, pre, and cal. In the discussion below, I append a _notes suffix to make it clear that I am using the DERA Notes (dera_notes) version of these tables. I also use num_notes for examples focused on Costco’s 2025 10-K.

3.2 Submissions data

The sub_notes table contains data on submissions, including the firm identifier (cik), the SIC code (sic), the period for the filing (period), the type of filings (form) and the timestamp on which the filing was accepted (accepted). In this note, I focus exclusively on 10-K filings (10-K), ignoring amended 10-K filings (10-K/A), filings by foreign issuers (e.g., 20-F) and transition filings (10-KT). While Table 1 shows that there are other annual report forms on sub_notes, 10-K filings are the vast majority of filings.8

3.3 The case of Costco’s 2025 10-K filing

The accession number for Costco’s 2025 10-K filing is 0000909832-25-000101. Table 2 shows some selected data from sub_notes for this filing.9 Looking at accepted in Table 2 causes me to wonder if a filing after 9pm EDT is normal for Costco.10 As it is quite trivial to address this question using the dera.pq data, I create Table 3. From Table 3, it does seem that most filings are after standard business hours for the US East Coast (Costco is headquartered in the US/Pacific time zone), but rarely as late as 9pm EDT.

Table 3 also includes lag, which is the time (in days) between period and accepted. Interestingly, there’s no clear pattern to lag over time, though it does appear to be trending downwards and to vary from one year to the next.

3.3.1 Presentation data

I now turn to the pre_notes table, which provides information about the presentation of XBRL data in the associated SEC filing. The variable stmt describes the statement to which each row of the data relates. Table 4 provides information about the distribution of this variable across all 10-K filings on the DERA Notes data.

As might be expected, most data items are not associated with any statement (i.e., stmt is NA), presumably because they are contained in the footnotes to the financial statements. Comparing Table 4 with Table 1 confirms that the vast majority of 10-K filings have all four core financial statements (i.e., BS, CF, IS, and EQ) on pre_notes. Further research would be required to understand whether the cases without one or more of these financial statements can be attributed to the absence of these statements in the filings, absence from the XBRL data supplied with the filing, or some failure in the extraction of the data into the pre_notes data supplied with DERA Notes.

The fields I use from pre_notes are adsh, line, tag, plabel, prole, and negating. To better understand the content of pre_notes, I present a sample of the underlying data in Table 5 ordered by line (for reasons of space, I omit adsh; it is always 0000909832-25-000101 here). As can be seen there, each line is associated with a tag and negating will be used to handle the values I get from cal_notes in later tables.

For my analysis here, the only use I make of prole is to filter out the difficult-to-handle row where prole equals “periodStartLabel”.11

Due to space constraints, I present plabel values in a separate table Table 6. The SEC DERA documentation describes plabel as “the text presented on the line item, also known as a ‘preferred’ label”. That plabel is a little more human-readable can be seen in Table 6.

3.3.2 Numeric data

The num_notes table contains numeric data, one row per data point in the financial statements and the notes thereto. Table 7 provides a sample of such data related to Costco’s 2025 statement of cash flows. Note that the values in uom (the unit of measure) do not vary within this Costco data set. However, qtrs does vary: while most items on Costco’s statement of cash flows have qtrs equal to 4, the value of qtrs for beginning and ending balances of cash and cash equivalents equals 0, as these are point-in-time balances.12

Note that I filter for iprx == 0, where iprx is “a positive integer to distinguish different reported facts that otherwise would have the same primary key. For most purposes, data with iprx greater than 1 are not needed.” I also filter for dimn == 0, as values with dimn greater than zero in this context represent segment data.

Table 7 also includes data on datp. According to the DERA Notes documentation datp represents “the difference between the reported fact date and the month-end rounded date (ddate), expressed as a fraction of 1. For example, a fact reported for 29/Dec, with ddate rounded to 31/Dec, has a datp value of minus 2/31 = -0.0645.” This provides a nice illustration of the reality that, pace the views of RTFM proponents, one should not always trust what one Reads in The Flaming Manual. If you look at the 10-K for Costco, you will see references to “August 31, 2025” (i.e., 0 days before 31 August), “September 1, 2024” (-1 days before 31 August), and “September 3, 2023” (-3 days before 31 August) and these values line up with datp representing the number of days between the fiscal year-end and the month-end (31 August). The field tdate in Table 7 is not actually a field in num_notes, but is a column I calculate using ddate and datp.

Finally, the column dcml indicates the decimal precision of the value column, with -6 meaning the data are reported in millions of the units in uom (i.e., USD).

3.3.3 Merged data

Now that we understand the data in pre_notes and num_notes, we can merge the data for Costco’s 2025 10-K to produce Tables 8–11. I merge data from pre_notes with data from cal_notes using adsh and tag as the linking variables.13

I present the data from Costco’s 2025 statement of cash flows over several tables partly for reasons of space, starting with cash flows from operating activities in Table 8. I scale the data by dividing the values by one million and I multiply any value where negating is true by minus one.

From Table 7, it can be seen that the num_notes data are in a long form with ddate arranged in a single column, while the typical presentation of statements of cash flows will have the dates organized as columns. Therefore I use the pivot_wider() function to reorganize the data for presentation.14

Table 8 presents the first 12 lines of the merged data set, which relate to cash flows from operating activities. Costco’s cash flows from operating activities are larger than net income—due to the add-back of depreciation and amortization—and growing.

Table 9 presents the next six lines of the merged data set, which relate to cash flows from investing activities. The largest item is capital expenditure (“additions to property and equipment”). That capital expenditures are roughly double depreciation and amortization suggests that Costco is, despite its maturity, still a growing firm.

For reasons I would need to explain in a different note, for standard financial analysis purposes, cash flows would generally be categorized as either operating or financing, with investing cash flows for GAAP purposes relating either to operating activities (e.g., capital expenditures) or to financing activities (e.g., the purchase or sale of short-term investment securities).

A rough-and-ready measure of free cash flows can be calculated by subtracting capital expenditures from cash flows from operating activities. I say rough-and-ready because a more refined measure would do a better job of separating financing cash flows from operating cash flows. For example, it might be appropriate to view the tax withholding on stock-based awards or some portion of the financing lease payments—see Table 10—as operating cash flows despite their classification as financing cash flows in US GAAP.15 It is also probably appropriate to view the interest paid—see Table 11—as financing, not operating, as it is considered in US GAAP. Similarly, some portion of the income taxes paid could be allocated to financing (e.g., the tax savings on interest).

In the case of Costco, the small amount of interest paid means that the rough-and-ready measure will be quite close to a more refined measure.

3.3.4 Calculation data

Finally, I turn to the data in cal_notes, which has the fields adsh, grp, arc, negative, ptag, pversion, ctag, and cversion. Table 12 provides a sample of observations from that table for Costco’s 10-K filing. It turns out that rows with grp equal to 7 relate to the income statement, those with grp equal to 9 relate to the balance sheet and those with grp equal to 14 relate to the statement of comprehensive income. The rows related to the statement of cash flows have grp equal to 3. These rows are shown in Table 13.

From Table 12 and Table 13, it should be clear that the data in cal_notes describe a hierarchy of calculations, with ctag representing an item that is a “child” of the item represented by ptag (i.e., “parent tag”). I represent the hierarchy embedded in Table 13 in Figure 1.

In some ways, the calculation hierarchy in Figure 1 resembles a typical statement of cash flows, with an overall change in cash sitting at the root of the tree as the parent of the tags representing cash flows from operating activities, investing activities, financing activities and the effect of exchange rate changes. This second level of the tree then has child nodes representing more detailed cash flow items. Each item can be aggregated up the tree to produce the cash flow totals at the higher levels, with due note being taken of negative in doing so.

Looking at Figure 1, we can see also that Costco uses three custom tags in its cash flow calculations. In each case, it seems Costco has combined multiple standard taxonomy items into a single line. FinancingLeasePaymentsAndOtherFinancingActivitiesNet seems to combine FinanceLeasePrincipalPayments, which covers the principal portion of finance lease payments, and “other” financing cash flows.16 ImpairmentOfAssetsAndOtherNonCashOperatingActivitiesNet combines AssetImpairmentCharges and some catch-all item like OtherNoncashIncomeExpense. OperatingandFinancingLeaseRightofUseAssetAmortization presumably combines amortization of both operating and financing leases.17

While it seems clear that Costco has used custom tags to combine multiple standard taxonomy items to align with its chosen presentation in the statement of cash flows, the use of custom tags does have the unfortunate effect of making it more difficult to compare the cash flow data across firms, as other firms may not make the same presentation choices and therefore may use standard US GAAP tags or use different custom tags. In cases like this, perhaps the better approach would be to require the use of standard tags at a lower level but allow firms to aggregate amounts using custom tags purely for presentation purposes.

3.4 Population analysis

Now that we have a better understanding of the data from examination of Costco’s statements of cash flows, I move on to an analysis of the broader population of 10-K filings on DERA Notes.

3.4.1 Calculation data

Recall that my goal is the classification of the operating portions of statements of cash flows based on tags used for operating cash flows in the XBRL data available in DERA Notes. For filings like that provided by Costco’s 2025 10-K filing, it seems that using the data in cal_notes will provide the most reliable approach. In effect, all I have to do is to start at the top of the hierarchy of calculation relationships and then work down to identify the tag that represents cash flows from operating activities and then to identify the tags that are children of that tag in the calculation hierarchy.

Once I have identified the tags representing cash flows from operating activities, it should be fairly straightforward to link specific tags to either the direct-method presentation or the indirect-method presentation.18

For this reason, my approach to classifying the presentation method of each filing prioritizes cal_notes data where available and reliable.

Unfortunately, not all filings have cal_notes data and, of those that do, not all have a cal structure that is regular enough to allow for the reliable identification of the operating-section leaf-node tags.

Table 14 provides periods from 2009 through 2025 and filings on Form 10-K. On the basis of Table 14, I focus on periods between 2014 and 2025, because earlier filings do not reliably include cal_notes data and 2026 is incomplete at the time of writing.19

Table 15 provides information on the regularity of the structure for filings with available cal data. Using recursive common table expressions (CTEs), I create trees for each filing on cal_notes and classify the structure of the cal_notes data for each filing as either regular or irregular, with regularity defined in terms of the presence of an operating cash flow node alongside nodes for investing and financing cash flows.

For filings classified as regular in Table 15, I use operating-section leaf-node tags identified through the cal_notes linkbase to determine the reporting method, as shown in Table 16.

3.4.2 Presentation data

For the remaining filings—those lacking cal_notes data or with irregular cal_notes data—I fall back on pre_notes data, applying the same tag-based direct- versus indirect-method classification to all cash flow statement tags on pre_notes. A filing is unclassifiable under this approach if none of its cash flow tags are recognisable as either direct- or indirect-method tags. Table 17 reports the results of this pre_notes-based classification; the “No cal data” and “Irregular” columns match the corresponding entries in Table 14 and Table 15, respectively.

Table 19 shows that almost all 10-K filings have some data on pre_notes. However, a small percentage of 10-K filings have no data for one or more of the key statements: balance sheet (“No BS data”), income statement (“No IS data”), or cash flows (“No CF data”). The “No FS data” indicates the number of cases where there is no data on pre_notes for any of these three key financial statements. Table 19 also suggests that the availability of filings on pre_notes is high from 2011 onwards, apart from a blip in 2013. The 2026 data cover only part of the year, so relatively few firms have filed 10-Ks for periods ending in calendar 2026.

From Table 20, we see that there are 706 filings with no CF data on pre_notes during the period I focus on (2014–2025). All of the cases are either irregular or no-cal filings, so they fall within the 971 unclassifiable cases in Table 17. For these filings, with no usable data from either cal_notes or pre_notes, there is no systematic basis for classification.
