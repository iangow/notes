  1. Given the above, why do you think we included fit_test_score_panel() rather than using pf.feols()? Develop and run a test of your hypothesis for why we did so.

The likely reason is speed. The chapter runs the same two-way fixed-effects regression many times in simulation, and fit_test_score_panel() is specialized to this exact setting. The following code compares the two approaches over a modest number of replications.

import time

num_runs = 50
sim_dfs = [
    get_test_scores(effect_size=0, seed=i)
    for i in range(num_runs)
]

start = time.perf_counter()
_ = [
    pf.feols(
        "score ~ I(treat * post) | grade + id",
        data=df,
        vcov="iid",
    ).coef()["I(treat * post)"]
    for df in sim_dfs
]
pyfixest_time = time.perf_counter() - start

start = time.perf_counter()
_ = [
    fit_test_score_panel(df, vcov="iid").item(0, "estimate")
    for df in sim_dfs
]
helper_time = time.perf_counter() - start

pl.DataFrame({
    "method": ["pyfixest", "fit_test_score_panel"],
    "seconds": [pyfixest_time, helper_time],
    "runs": [num_runs, num_runs],
}).with_columns(
    seconds_per_run=pl.col("seconds") / pl.col("runs")
).with_columns(
    pl.col("seconds", "seconds_per_run").round(4)
).style

The timing table measures the relative speed of the helper and the general estimator in this run. The exact times depend on the computer, software versions and workload. That speed matters because the chapter uses 1,000 replications in several settings.

2 Discussion questions

  1. The proprietary cost hypothesis tested by Li et al. (2018) posits that increased cost of disclosure will lead to reduced supply of disclosure. What rival theories exist that would make alternative predictions? Are there other elements of disclosure theory that might be tested? Why do you think Li et al. (2018) focused on this specific element of the proprietary cost hypothesis?

There are no clear rival theories other than “no effect”. Of course, “no effect” is unlikely to be found in a published paper for reasons discussed in Chapter 19. There are other elements of the theory that might be examined, such as the firms with good news (observed in later realizations) being the ones who disclose and the market reacting favourably to disclosure. At times it seems the least interesting aspect of “proprietary cost hypothesis” gets studied again and again in “increasingly clever” ways.

  1. In the analysis above, we do not include the control variables considered by Li et al. (2018). For example, Li et al. (2018) “include R&D expenditures to sales, advertisement [sic] expenditure to sales, and intangible assets scaled by total assets to control for a firm’s proprietary costs of disclosure.” Using the approach outlined in Chapter 4, in what circumstances would it be necessary to control for “a firm’s proprietary costs of disclosure” in this way? Do these circumstances seem applicable in this setting, where the treatment is a (presumably exogenous) shock to disclosure costs and the outcome is disclosure choices?

The first question is whether a proposed control is a pre-treatment common cause, a precision variable, or a variable affected by IDD. Controlling for proprietary costs changed by IDD could block the proposed mechanism. A better interpretation is simply that the wording used by Li et al. (2018) is poor. Instead, Li et al. (2018) are in effect using IDD adoption as an exogenous shock to the treatment. This is like using IDD adoption as an instrument, but focused on intent-to-treat analyses.1

Intent-to-treat analyses are common when researchers have an instrument, but do not observe the treatment of interest directly.2 In this setting, the treatment of interest is “proprietary costs of disclosure” and a maintained assumption is that IDD adoption is random, has a direct causal effect on proprietary costs of disclosure, and does not affect the outcome (in this setting, voluntary disclosure of customer identities) except through its effect on the treatment. This can be represented on a causal diagram as in Figure 1.

In conventional IV analyses, it is considered a standard step to evaluate the relationship between the instrument (\(Z\)) and the treatment (\(X\)) statistically.3 But this is not possible when the treatment is not observed. As such, in addition to assumptions about the causal relationship between \(Z\) and \(X\), the magnitude of the proprietary-cost change is unobserved. A reduced-form effect of IDD exposure does not require dividing by a measured first stage, but it does not identify the effect per unit of proprietary costs. So we need to assume that IDD adoption has a causal effect on proprietary costs of disclosure and the empirical tests are joint tests of that assumption and the hypothesis that increased costs of disclosure lead to reduced supply of disclosure.4

The variables that need to be controlled for are any determinants of “a firm’s proprietary costs of disclosure” that also affect the instrument. If IDD exposure were randomly assigned, baseline controls could improve precision. If it is only as-if random conditional on measured factors, those factors matter for identification; time-varying confounders and conditional parallel trends remain relevant. Post-treatment measures of proprietary costs should not be added mechanically. But Li et al. (2018) no doubt understood that in accounting research it’s important to “control for stuff”.

  1. Would you expect the inclusion of controls (see the question above) to have a significant impact on the regression results? Why or why not?

I would not expect the controls to have a big impact for a number of reasons. First, many controls would be fairly persistent over time and, with firm fixed effects included, only within-firm variation in controls that is correlated with the treatment indicator (i.e., POST for treatment firms) will affect the estimated effects.

With firm fixed effects, only within-firm variation in controls can change the treatment estimate. Persistence makes a small effect plausible when the remaining variation is weakly related to treatment, but it is not a guarantee. A time-varying omitted confounder can still matter. State effects provide a useful sensitivity check because firms sometimes change headquarters state; the paper includes state effects in its specification. When making that comparison, hold the estimation sample and covariance convention fixed first, so that changes in the coefficient are not confused with changes in the sample or standard errors.

  1. What patterns do you observe in the coefficients reported in Table 4? Do these conform to what you would expect from Li et al. (2018)? (It may be easiest to focus on these in groups, e.g., those in \(t-5\) to \(t-2\), those in \(t + 0\) to \(t+3\) and those for \(t+4\) and \(t+5\).)

In Table 4, most pre-adoption coefficients are negative, while the clearest positive signal in the full sample is in the pooled \(t+5\)-and-later bin. The weighted measure is only marginally significant at \(t+4\); results for firms observed to switch differ. The estimates and their uncertainty should be read together, rather than treating a sign as evidence of a nonzero effect.

These do not conform with what I’d expect from reading Li et al. (2018). First, the negative coefficients in years \(t-5\) through \(t-2\) are problematic, but their signs alone do not establish anticipation or rejection of parallel trends. Their uncertainty and a joint test of the leads should also be examined. Second, the clearest positive signals appear relatively late in this expanded specification. Li et al. (2018) discuss effects within one or two years [pp. 269 and 288], so it is inaccurate to say that the paper does not discuss delay. Table 3 pools observations from \(t+2\) onward and cannot identify onset within that pooled period. Online appendix Table OA2 uses a \([-2,+2]\) window and reports significant coefficients at exactly \(t+2\), with a matched sample and a different reference year. Our later pattern is a discrepancy to investigate, rather than a direct contradiction of estimates from that different design.

The number and composition of firms vary by event year. The later coefficients rely on fewer firms, and a pooled tail can contain several observations from each firm. Report firm counts as well as row counts before judging how much independent support a late-period estimate has.

  1. How do the variables in Table 4 differ from those used in Table 3 of Li et al. (2018)? Modify the code above (e.g., format_event_time()) to produce analysis closer to that reported in Table 3 of Li et al. (2018).

Li et al. (2018) treat years before \(t-2\) the same as years without treatment and collapse years after \(t+2\) into year \(t+2\). We can replace the regression above by using a modified format_event_time() as follows. This function pools the years before \(t - 2\) with the control firms by setting their level at \(-\infty\) and caps the upper event-time indicator at \(t + 2\).

The other thing that Li et al. (2018) do is report coefficients relative to non-treatment firms and firm-years strictly before \(t-2\) (some reference group needs to be chosen), which we implement by pooling pre-\(t-2\) treated firm-years with the control group.

def format_event_time_llz(value):
    if value is None or value < -2:
        return -np.inf
    value = int(value)
    return float(min(2, value))

reg_data_t_llz = (
    reg_data
    .join(switch_years, on="gvkey", how="left")
    .with_columns(
        (pl.col("year") - pl.col("adoption_year"))
        .map_elements(
            format_event_time_llz,
            return_dtype=pl.Float64,
            skip_nulls=False,
        )
        .alias("t_llz"),
    )
)

fms_llz = [
    pf.feols("ln_ratio ~ C(t_llz, Treatment(reference=-np.inf)) "
             "| gvkey + year",
             data=reg_data_t_llz, vcov="iid"),
    pf.feols("ln_ratio_sale ~ C(t_llz, Treatment(reference=-np.inf)) "
             "| gvkey + year",
             data=reg_data_t_llz, vcov="iid"),
]

The positive pooled coefficients in Table 6 have the same broad direction as Table 3 of Li et al. (2018). These regressions retain IID standard errors and change the event coding only, so their significance cannot be described as stronger evidence than the paper’s differently clustered specification. The net effect of the reporting choices made here appears to be to collapse the effects of \(t+3\), \(t+4\), and \(t+5\) into \(t+2\) and to suppress the reporting of any of the awkward negative coefficients in years \(t-5\) through \(t-3\).

This changes the event coding, but it does not reproduce the paper’s full specification. We retain the book’s controls, missing-value handling and first observed pre-to-post transition as the event year. That transition can reflect a headquarters change or an observation gap, whereas the paper’s indicators refer to state adoption years. The authors’ supplied code replaces missing weighted ratios with one; our code retains them as missing. State effects, state-clustered inference and sample restrictions are further distinct choices.

A significant coefficient on the pooled \(t+2\)-and-later category does not establish that an effect begins at exactly \(t+2\). It averages the later years too, while pooling early years into the reference removes their separate estimates from view. Assess the displayed comparison with these differences in mind.

  1. The format_event_time() function collapses years after \(t + 5\) and before \(t - 5\) into years \(t + 5\) and \(t - 5\), respectively. When would this approach make sense? What would be one alternative approach to handling these years? Does your suggested approach make a difference in this setting?

Binning can make sense when very early and late event years contain few observations, so separate estimates would be unstable and hard to interpret. Pooling gives more precise tail estimates, but imposes a common effect on all years in each tail. It is most defensible when that approximation is plausible and the research question concerns the years near adoption. It can conceal changes or reversals in distant years, so the bin definitions and sensitivity to them should be reported.

One alternative approach would be not to collapse them at all and report them separately. Here we use the format_event_time_all() function defined above, which implements this idea.

def format_event_time_all(value):
    if value is None:
        return -np.inf
    return float(int(value))

reg_data_t_all = (
    reg_data
    .join(switch_years, on="gvkey", how="left")
    .with_columns(
        (pl.col("year") - pl.col("adoption_year"))
        .map_elements(
            format_event_time_all,
            return_dtype=pl.Float64,
            skip_nulls=False,
        )
        .alias("t_all"),
    )
)

fms_all_years = [
    pf.feols("ln_ratio ~ C(t_all, Treatment(reference=-1)) "

same_state is true when the headquarters state is unchanged from the preceding observed record and false when the transition coincides with a recorded state change. The first-transition calculation follows the book’s event-year convention; the “at least one” calculation answers a different firm-level question.

The changed-state cases weaken the claim that every exposure change comes from an exogenous court ruling in a firm’s existing state. They can reflect endogenous relocation, but location-record changes, mergers and other events may also explain them; these records alone do not establish intentional relocation. They may add statistical variation while making causal interpretation harder. Useful sensitivity checks restrict transitions to unchanged-state firms or fix headquarters location before the ruling, while acknowledging the resulting selection and measurement issues.

One issue is that the sample is changing from one event-period to the next. Requiring observation at \(t+4\) does not ensure a balanced panel at all earlier event times, and it excludes the never-switching comparison pool. A balanced-window sensitivity analysis must explicitly require each desired event year and separately retain an appropriate control pool. One idea would be to require that a firm be in the sample in \(t + 4\) (the full and switcher samples play different roles once year effects are included) and to drop all periods after \(t + 4\) and run the by-year regressions for this sample.

The \(t+4\) restriction also selects on later survival and data availability. A fully balanced event window would require exactly one complete observation in every chosen event year for both outcomes, then retain an appropriate comparison pool. Report the number of qualifying treated firms and support by adoption cohort: balancing can sharply reduce the available sample. Neither balancing nor the selected \(t+4\) sample resolves heterogeneous treatment effects in a staggered-adoption design. The changing sample, a sample selected at \(t+4\), and a balanced-window sample answer different questions.
